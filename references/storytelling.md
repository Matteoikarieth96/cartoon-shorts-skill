# Storytelling for shorts

What worked and what failed while making several cartoon series (sport stories, history, recipes, product explainers).

## The first 3 seconds

- Open on the payoff or the contrast, not on the setup. "5000 to 1" on screen before anyone explains odds.
  "Ready in 30 min" struck out, "Done in 12" slams in. The dish name, the time and the cost in frame 0.
- The subject must be readable with the sound off, in frame 0. No logo sting, no title card that fades in.
- A plain question in everyday words works as line 1 ("Cooking pasta tonight?"). Jargon does not.
- If everyone already knows the ending (a famous quote, a famous win), open with that ending and rewind
  ("They called him the greatest... ten years earlier, nobody knew his name"). The loop closes at the end.

## One thread, not a chronicle

- A short is one story with one emotional line. "First night, second night, third night" plus scores is a
  chronicle and feels like separate moments. Pick a thread (a doubt, a promise, a name, a number that changes)
  and make every scene move it.
- Three key moments are enough for 60 s. Each one changes the thread (the odds shrink, the promise is tested).
- Give it a turn: "But..." Something must be at stake or doubted. No antagonist and no doubt means no story.
- Do not win before the story begins: if the setup already promises the happy ending, there is nothing to watch.
- Show the payoff on screen (the cup lifted, the plate served, the letter read). Do not bury it under an epilogue.

## A structure that works for explainers and true stories

1. A yes/no question or a striking claim, spoken first.
2. "Well," the premise with a year and a humble origin.
3. Two or three dated steps, each on screen as a small card.
4. One everyday analogy that makes the idea click.
5. "But" the turn.
6. The question again, and a dry, short answer.
7. A surprise kicker that closes the loop with line 1, then the call to action.

## Endings

- A moral must be indirect: show the image that carries it, never a narrator saying "the lesson is...".
- How-tos end with a recap card the viewer can screenshot (everything on one screen, held about 5 s),
  then a short ask ("Comment a country or a dish").
- Series end with the same ritual every time, so viewers learn it.

## Writing the lines

- 8 to 14 words per line. One idea per line. Spoken words, not written ones.
- Numbers that matter go on screen as big type, and the voice says them once.
- Give each line a delivery note (`inst`): "whisper, then smile", "announce like a sports anchor",
  "slow, every word weighs". The same text with a different note sounds like a different scene.
- Names the voice may mispronounce: add a pronunciation guide in the note ("Amatrice = ah-MAH-tree-cheh").
- Captions can be shorter than the voice; highlight 1 or 2 key words per line with `*stars*`.

## Making it look alive

- Something moves in every frame: a slow camera push, breathing characters, blinking eyes, drifting dust.
- Change the picture every 2 to 3 s: a new card, a cut, a zoom, a character expression.
- Save shakes, flashes and impact frames for the 2 or 3 biggest beats. Everywhere is nowhere.
- Food, faces and the hero object deserve the most drawing time: they are what people stop for.

## Judging a draft

Write 2 or 3 different structures. For each, one line: the hook, the thread, the payoff. If subagents are
available, let 3 independent judges score them for hook strength, clarity of the thread and emotional payoff,
then show the user the winner and the runner-up. When the user says "the storytelling does not work",
change the structure, not the drawings.
